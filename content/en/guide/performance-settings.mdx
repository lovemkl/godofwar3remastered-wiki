## The Three Settings That Matter

Strip the menu down and three decisions control almost everything about how the God of War 3 Remastered PC port looks and runs:

**Output resolution.** The port renders the remaster's assets at your chosen output, up to full 4K. The gap between 1080p and 4K on this material is larger than you might expect — the remaster's art was built to survive scrutiny, and high resolutions reward it. If your hardware can hold your target frame rate at native resolution, take it.

**Frame rate ceiling.** With the cap raised, capable hardware pushes the port to 120, 144 and up to 240 FPS. Frame rate is not cosmetic in this game: Kratos' combat is choreography — dodge windows, grab timing, quick-time events — and the original's 30-60 FPS console cadence hid detail that high refresh surfaces. Set the ceiling to your display's refresh rate, no lower.

**FSR upscaling.** AMD's FidelityFX Super Resolution renders the scene at a lower internal resolution and reconstructs it to your output, trading a little sharpness for a lot of frames. For GPUs near the demonstrated 4GB VRAM floor of the God of War 3 Remastered PC port, FSR is the difference between 30-something and genuinely fluid play. The quality presets matter — see the tier recipes below.

Everything else in the menu is seasoning. Texture and shadow options exist and are worth a look on older cards, but the three decisions above are the meal; the options you can safely ignore are cataloged on the features page so this page stays focused.

## Hardware Tiers, Tested

The configurations below reflect the port's first weeks of real-world coverage — independent testing and creator benchmarks, not vendor claims. Find the tier that matches your machine and start there.

**The 4GB VRAM floor.** Early coverage demonstrated the port running on a 4GB VRAM GPU, and that is the honest floor, not the comfortable middle. The recipe: 1080p output, FSR on at quality preset, frame ceiling 60. You are not getting the port's showpiece numbers at this tier — you are getting playable Kratos on hardware that emulators would reject outright, which is exactly what a native port is for.

**The mid-range sweet spot.** Independent testing on an RX 6700 XT with a Ryzen 5 5600 and 32GB of RAM reported smooth play — and this is the tier most players actually own. The recipe: 1440p native (or 4K with FSR quality), frame ceiling matched to a 120-144Hz display, everything else default. Mid-range hardware carries this port the way it carries well-made native games: without drama.

**Modern flagships.** RTX-class cards and current AMD flagships were shown pushing the port toward its advertised 240 FPS ceiling in launch-window coverage. The recipe: native 4K, ceiling at your display's maximum, FSR off — you bought the headroom, spend it. A high-refresh 4K display is the only boundary this tier respects.

**SteamOS and handhelds.** Coverage demonstrated the port running under SteamOS, which quietly makes it a handheld-class option for devices with enough GPU. Expect the 4GB-tier recipe with a handheld's thermal limits in mind; the port's low floor is doing the heavy lifting.

## The 240 FPS Question, Answered Honestly

The port's headline number deserves a paragraph of honesty. Yes, the ceiling reaches 240 FPS; yes, launch-window coverage showed high-end cards running far past console cadence. But the number you experience is the product of your GPU, your output resolution, your FSR choice and your display — in that order. A 240Hz monitor without flagship hardware should run 1440p with a modest FSR preset and will deliver an experience no PS4 owner ever had. Chase the settings that hold your refresh rate stable rather than a screenshot number; the features page's 4K-and-240FPS breakdown covers the theory for those who want it.

One quirk worth knowing: like many high-frame-rate ports, the game's difficulty was calibrated for its original cadence, and combat can feel subtly faster at very high frame rates because animations interpolate more smoothly. If a quick-time event window feels different from memory, it is the frame rate talking. This is normal, desirable, and one more reason the ceiling matters.

## FSR Presets Without the Marketing

FSR presets are named for their internal render scale, and the practical differences are simple. Quality keeps the most detail and costs the most performance — start here. Balanced is the middle child most mid-range cards settle on at 4K output. Performance is for the floor tier reaching upward, and the sharpness cost starts showing. A useful mental model: FSR buys frames with resolution, so pair the preset with the output you actually chose — FSR quality at 1080p output is a waste of the feature, FSR performance at 4K on a 4GB card is survival. Tune one step at a time and let an hour of play, not a benchmark minute, tell you where to stop.

## When Performance Is Wrong

If your results miss your tier's recipe by a wide margin, work the checklist in order: confirm the frame ceiling and vsync settings (ceilings below your display's refresh are the number one silent killer of frame rate); confirm FSR is actually enabled — its effect is easy to misread in the menu; check that your GPU is the one being used, not an integrated chip quietly rendering the game; and regenerate the config file as described on the install page if the menu's saved state looks corrupted. The port's own overhead is small; when it underperforms, the cause is almost always configuration, and the fix is almost always one of those four.
